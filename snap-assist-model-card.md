# Model Card for Nava Labs Benefits Navigator Chatbot

This model card describes the **ideal specification** for an LLM-based assistant that answers SNAP-related questions, based on Nava Labs' published account of how they designed their caseworker chatbot ("Building a trustworthy AI chatbot," Nava PBC, Oct. 22, 2025, and the companion case study "Refining an AI chatbot that cites its sources," Nov. 8, 2024), cross-referenced against the independent evaluation of that system in Gosciak et al., *"LLMs in social services: How does chatbot accuracy affect human accuracy?"* (arXiv:2603.11213, 2026). The intended users can broadly be called "caseworkers" — a category that spans call-center specialists, community health workers, and other community partners, sometimes referred to internally as "benefit navigators." This card is a synthesis for illustrative purposes; it is not an official model card issued by Nava, and no model weights are released.

## Model Details

### Model Description

The Benefits Navigator Chatbot is a **citation-grounded retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system**, not a fine-tuned model. Its design philosophy, per Nava, is that trustworthiness is achieved primarily through *engineering constraints and process*, not by improving the base LLM itself. Specifically:

- The chatbot is instructed to answer **only** from a specific, **hand-picked set of official source documents** — the same materials caseworkers themselves are trained on (e.g., state policy manuals such as the Maryland SNAP Policy Handbook and the MI Bridges Combined Policy Manual, or an organization's internal knowledge base such as Guru). It is explicitly instructed **not** to draw on its broader pretraining knowledge.
- If the hand-picked source material does not contain an answer, the chatbot is instructed to **say it doesn't know** rather than guess — a deliberate design choice to suppress hallucination rather than rely on the base model's general knowledge.
- Every generated answer is paired with **direct-quote source citations**, including links back to the original document, so a caseworker can verify the claim in context rather than trusting the summary at face value.
- Citations are **not generated by the LLM as free text**. To prevent hallucinated citations (e.g., citing a nonexistent document), the system uses a two-step process: the generative model identifies *which* source document and paragraph(s) it drew on, and then the system **directly pulls the verbatim quote from the source** rather than letting the model write the quote itself.
- Use of the chatbot is **optional** for caseworkers at partner organizations; it is not mandated, and adoption is intended to spread through demonstrated trust and word of mouth rather than requirement.

### Model Architecture — Retrieval and Chunking

Nava iterated through three chunking strategies before settling on a hybrid approach, reflecting a direct tradeoff between answer quality (needs more context) and citation readability (needs short, coherent excerpts):

| Experiment | Chunking method | Effect on LLM answer quality | Effect on citation readability |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fixed-length chunks (~500 words, matching the embedding model's input limit) | Good — ample context for the LLM | Poor — citations often started/ended mid-paragraph, mixed topics, too long to scan quickly on a live call |
| 2 | Paragraph-based chunking (variable length, shorter) | Worse — less surrounding context made it harder for the LLM to select the most relevant chunk and to generate a good answer | Good — citations were coherent, single-topic, easy to read |
| 3 (adopted) | **Hybrid "long chunk / sub-chunk" approach:** larger chunks built around document structure (section headings) are sent to the LLM for answer generation; the LLM then identifies which paragraph-length **sub-chunks** within that larger chunk are most relevant, and only those sub-chunks are shown as the direct-quote citation | Good — full context preserved for generation | Good — citations stay short and topically coherent |

Additional retrieval-quality measures Nava reports:
- An **embedding model** scores each chunk of source material against the caseworker's question to find the most relevant material before sending it to the LLM; because the embedding model truncates input beyond roughly 500 words, source documents must be pre-split into chunks at or below that limit.
- Chunks are enriched with **metadata** (e.g., parent document headings, a summary of preceding paragraphs) so the LLM interprets each chunk with more surrounding context, rather than being given the chunk in isolation.
- Chunks are built by **analyzing document structure** (section headings) to produce cohesive sections, rather than splitting text at arbitrary fixed-length boundaries.

- **Developed by:** Nava Labs (Nava, a Public Benefit Corporation), in partnership with caseworker organizations, subject-matter experts, and families; publicly documented by Greg Jordan-Detamore, Kevin Boyer, and Ryan Hansz.
- **Model type:** Citation-grounded RAG assistant (general-purpose LLM generator + semantic-similarity retriever + non-generative citation extraction layer)
- **Language(s):** English (source documentation and examples shown are in English; the base study population served clients in multiple languages, but the chatbot's documented design is English-only)
- **License:** Not specified (proprietary; internal/partner deployment)
- **Base models referenced across sources:** GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, and Google Gemini (per the independent evaluation); Nava's own case studies describe the system generically as "an LLM" without committing to one vendor
- **Finetuned from model:** Not fine-tuned — behavior is controlled through source-document restriction, system instructions ("say you don't know"), and retrieval/chunking design rather than parameter updates

## Uses

### Direct Use

- Real-time support for caseworkers/benefit navigators **while on the phone or in-session with a client**, answering questions about program rules (e.g., SNAP eligibility, income treatment, deductions) drawn strictly from vetted policy manuals.
- Quickly locating and quoting the specific passage of a long (1,000+ page) policy manual relevant to a caseworker's question, replacing manual document search.
- Cross-checking a caseworker's own understanding: per Nava's user interviews, the tool was used both to **validate** existing caseworker knowledge and to **fill gaps** on questions caseworkers didn't know the answer to.

### Downstream Use

- Embeddable within other case-management products (e.g., Benefit Navigator) as a chatbot widget with citation display and feedback capture.
- Extensible to other public-benefit programs beyond SNAP (Nava's broader "benefit navigator" framing and related work, e.g. Maryland H.R. 1 work-reporting prototypes, suggest the same architecture is reused across programs) — but each new program requires its own hand-picked, vetted source corpus; the design does not generalize automatically to unvetted domains.

### Out-of-Scope Use

- **Answering from general/pretrained knowledge.** The system is explicitly designed to refuse (say "I don't know") rather than answer from the LLM's broader training data if the hand-picked source corpus lacks the answer — using it to answer general knowledge questions defeats its trust design.
- **Cross-jurisdiction use without a matched source corpus.** Because policy is state-specific, the design explicitly guards against — for example — giving a California caseworker information sourced from a Texas Medicaid manual; the corpus must match the caseworker's jurisdiction.
- **Fully autonomous, non-reviewed client communication.** The tool is built for caseworkers, who are expected to read and verify the citation before relaying an answer to a client, not for direct unsupervised client use.
- **Mandatory or sole-source workflows.** Nava's deployment model keeps the tool optional; the design does not assume or require caseworkers to rely on it exclusively, and this optionality is itself treated as a trust-building mechanism.

## Bias, Risks, and Limitations

- **Citation quality depends entirely on chunking design.** Nava's own experiments show that a naive implementation (fixed-length chunks) produces citations that are technically accurate but practically unreadable — split mid-paragraph, mixing topics — which could undermine the verification behavior the whole design depends on if not carefully engineered.
- **Retrieval can surface the wrong source even when the answer text sounds right.** Nava's own example: a navigator asking about the Earned Income Tax Credit received an answer that was actually sourced from Child Tax Credit material — a real, documented retrieval failure mode, catchable specifically *because* citations were shown.
- **Embedding-model input limits constrain design.** The ~500-word truncation limit of the embedding model used is a hard technical constraint shaping how source documents must be chunked; a different embedding model with a different limit would require re-tuning the whole chunking pipeline.
- **"Say I don't know" reduces but does not eliminate hallucination risk**, and depends on the LLM reliably recognizing the boundary of its provided context rather than blending in outside knowledge.
- **Feedback loop is reactive, not preventive.** Thumbs-up/down and free-text feedback (e.g., a caseworker flagging "policy is out-of-date") help Nava identify and fix problems after they reach users, rather than preventing an initial bad answer.
- **Trust and calibration issues persist even with citations.** The independent evaluation (arXiv:2603.11213) found that, in a controlled study using suggestions of comparable accuracy to Nava's chatbot (though *without* citations), caseworkers could not reliably distinguish correct from incorrect AI suggestions (perception–accuracy correlation ≈ 0.04) and exhibited an "AI underreliance plateau" — underusing even highly accurate suggestions. Nava's citation-based design is intended to counteract this, but the controlled accuracy figures available (85% multiple-choice / 76% free-text, from the independent study) were measured on a version of the system's outputs *without* the citation feature, so citations' effect on end-user accuracy has not been isolated in a controlled trial.
- **Source corpus must be actively maintained.** Because the model refuses to answer outside its hand-picked corpus, an out-of-date manual produces either wrong answers or unnecessary "I don't know" responses — corpus staleness was flagged directly by a caseworker in Nava's own feedback data.
- **Vendor/base-model choice not fixed.** Nava's own materials describe the system generically as "an LLM"; the specific base model in production is not committed to in either source, which means downstream behavior (tone, refusal calibration, instruction-following fidelity) may vary by deployment or over time as Nava swaps providers.

### Recommendations

- Preserve the two-part answer contract (summary + non-generative, directly-pulled verbatim citation) — do not let the LLM free-write citations, per Nava's explicit design rationale.
- Use the hybrid long-chunk/sub-chunk retrieval pattern rather than either pure fixed-length or pure paragraph chunking, to avoid trading off answer quality against citation readability.
- Enrich chunks with structural metadata (parent headings, preceding-paragraph summaries) before sending to the generator.
- Keep the source corpus restricted to a hand-picked, jurisdiction-matched, actively maintained set of official documents; do not allow open-web or general-knowledge fallback.
- Maintain a lightweight feedback mechanism (thumbs up/down + free text) and route flagged content (e.g., stale policy) back into corpus maintenance.
- Keep adoption opt-in and pair with ongoing user research, consistent with Nava's stated trust-building approach — do not assume citation display alone is sufficient without also validating end-user calibration, given open questions raised by the independent evaluation.

## How to Get Started with the Model

Not applicable — no checkpoint is released. An implementer following this design would: (1) select a source corpus of official, jurisdiction-specific policy documents matching the caseworkers' own training materials; (2) chunk documents using the hybrid long-chunk/sub-chunk method, adding structural metadata; (3) build an embedding-based retriever scoring chunks against incoming questions; (4) prompt a general-purpose LLM to generate a summary answer strictly from retrieved context, explicitly instructed to decline if the context is insufficient; (5) have the LLM identify (not quote) the specific source chunk/paragraph used, and pull the citation text programmatically rather than generatively; (6) add a feedback capture UI (thumbs up/down + comment) surfaced below each answer; (7) pilot as an opt-in tool with ongoing caseworker research before wider rollout.

## Training Details

### Training Data

No fine-tuning dataset is used per Nava's description; behavior is controlled via retrieval corpus and prompting. The **retrieval corpus** is a hand-picked set of official documents matching what caseworkers are formally trained on — examples cited include the Maryland SNAP Policy Handbook, the MI Bridges Combined Policy Manual, and organization-specific internal knowledge bases (e.g., Guru).

For **independent benchmark evaluation** (not part of Nava's own design process, but used to score system outputs), a separate 770-question, expert-vetted multiple-choice benchmark was built from U.S. Department of Agriculture SNAP Quality Control (QC) error data (2017–2022), covering California's CalFresh program specifically. See arXiv:2603.11213 for full methodology, and https://huggingface.co/datasets/cornellbgl/SNAP_scenarios for the benchmark dataset.

### Training Procedure

Not applicable (no fine-tuning). Development proceeded through iterative prototyping cycles including 75+ user research sessions with caseworkers, subject-matter experts, and families, and at least three documented experimental iterations on chunking strategy alone.

#### Preprocessing

- Source documents are segmented into large, structurally-coherent chunks (by section heading), which are further divided into paragraph-length sub-chunks.
- Each chunk is scored against incoming questions via an embedding-model similarity comparison (embedding model has a ~500-word input limit, which sets the practical chunk-size ceiling).
- Chunk metadata (parent headings, preceding-paragraph summaries) is attached before chunks are passed to the generator.

## Evaluation

### Testing Data, Factors & Metrics

Nava's own published materials report **user satisfaction**, not accuracy benchmarks:

- **Metric:** Net-Promoter-style recommendation score ("How likely are you to recommend the chatbot to a colleague?", 0–10 scale)
- **Result:** Median score of **8** among piloting caseworkers.
- **Qualitative feedback:** Caseworkers reported the tool built trust in two ways — validating existing knowledge and filling knowledge gaps on complex questions. One caseworker: *"it is great to have something that gives you the info right away"* and valued that *"it gives you the source of where you're getting the info from."* Comparing it to web search, one caseworker said they trusted the chatbot more than Google because Google results could be outdated (e.g., from 2020) and untrustworthy by comparison.

For **independent accuracy evaluation**, see arXiv:2603.11213, which separately measured the chatbot's answer accuracy against expert-verified ground truth (not user satisfaction):

| Evaluation condition | Accuracy |
|---|---|
| Multiple-choice benchmark questions (n=770 pool) | 85% |
| Multiple-choice questions matching the caseworker study subset | 76%–82% |
| Free-text/open-ended version of the same questions (n=89) | 76% |

Unassisted caseworkers scored 49% on the same multiple-choice benchmark; random guessing would be expected to score 20–25%.

See also Public Benefits Bench v1.1 (https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/public-benefits-bench) which has found accuracy on SNAP question-answering to range from 50% to 76%.

#### Summary

Nava's own evaluation focuses on **trust and adoption** (satisfaction score, qualitative interviews), consistent with their stated design goal of trustworthiness rather than raw accuracy alone. The independent academic evaluation instead measured **answer correctness** against an expert-verified benchmark, and additionally studied how the chatbot's accuracy affects the accuracy of the humans using it. The two evaluations are complementary but were conducted separately, so it is not established from public sources whether the specific citation/chunking design described here was present during the independent accuracy testing.

## Environmental Impact

Not reported. As a RAG system built on commercial API-based LLMs plus a maintained embedding index (rather than custom pretraining), inference-time API usage and periodic re-embedding of policy corpora are the likely dominant compute costs; no figures are provided to estimate emissions.

## Technical Specifications

### Model Architecture and Objective

1. **Corpus curation:** Hand-picked, jurisdiction-matched official policy documents (state manuals, internal knowledge bases), explicitly excluding reliance on the LLM's general pretraining knowledge.
2. **Chunking:** Hybrid long-chunk (for generation context) + paragraph-length sub-chunk (for citation display) approach, built from document structure rather than fixed character counts, enriched with heading/summary metadata.
3. **Retrieval:** Embedding-model similarity scoring between the caseworker's question and each chunk (embedding model input capped at ~500 words).
4. **Generation:** General-purpose instruction-following LLM produces a plain-language summary answer strictly from retrieved chunks, and separately identifies which source chunk(s)/paragraph(s) it relied on.
5. **Citation assembly (non-generative):** The system programmatically pulls the verbatim source text for the identified chunk(s) rather than letting the LLM write the quote, specifically to prevent citation hallucination.
6. **Refusal behavior:** If retrieved context doesn't support an answer, the model is instructed to state it doesn't know rather than answer from general knowledge.
7. **Feedback capture:** Thumbs-up/down plus free-text comment displayed below each answer, reviewed by the Nava team to identify and correct systematic errors.

## Glossary

- **SNAP:** Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (federal); known as **CalFresh** in California.
- **Benefit navigator:** Nava's umbrella term for caseworkers, call-center specialists, and community health workers who help families enroll in public benefits.
- **Chunk / sub-chunk:** A segment of source document text; in Nava's hybrid design, larger structurally-coherent "chunks" are used for answer generation while shorter paragraph-length "sub-chunks" within them are used for citation display.
- **AI underreliance plateau:** Term from the independent evaluation (arXiv:2603.11213) describing the pattern where human accuracy gains from AI assistance level off even as AI accuracy rises further, because users cannot reliably tell correct from incorrect suggestions.
